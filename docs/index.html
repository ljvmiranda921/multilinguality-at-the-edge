Read the full abstract

Where and how language models (LMs) are deployed determines who can benefit from them. However, there are several challenges that prevent effective deployment of LMs in non-English-speaking and hardware-constrained communities in the Global South. We call this challenge the last mile: the intersection of multilinguality and edge deployment, where the goals are aligned but with competing technical requirements. We define the last mile along the dimensions of digital infrastructure and language capability, and describe the requirements and constraints they impose for developing and deploying accessible language technologies. To understand the state of the art and the challenges at this intersection, we survey 306 papers from 2021 to 2026 that tackle this problem across the language modelling pipeline, from data collection to development and deployment. We also discuss open questions and provide actionable recommendations for different stakeholders in the NLP ecosystem. Finally, we hope that this work contributes to a renewed understanding of what inclusive and equitable language technologies require to deliver meaningful benefit to the communities they serve.

What shapes multilingual edge LM development, and how can these models reach the communities that need them the most?

Communities with the highest linguistic diversity often face the most severe infrastructure constraints. Countries such as Papua New Guinea, Nigeria, and Chad are linguistically diverse yet among the least active on the Internet.

Answering this requires combining multilinguality and edge NLP. But combining the two areas is hard: while their goals are aligned, their technical requirements often compete, and the two research communities remain largely siloed. The field has several names for this tension: the low-resource double bind (Ahia et al., 2021), the square-one bias (Ruder et al., 2022), Zeno's paradox of language technology (Nigatu et al., 2024), among others.

Analysis: From Methods to Systems

We also looked into edge LM systems, which we define as completed efforts that have been integrated into real-world applications. To identify them, we manually classified each of the 306 papers on whether an actual model deployment took place, obtaining 58 systems in the process.

How are edge LMs developed?

To examine how edge LM systems are made, we situate the 58 deployment papers within the broader 306 surveyed papers. We embed each abstract with MiniLM, reduce to 2D with UMAP, and cluster with HDBSCAN. Then, we use KeyBERT to extract the top keywords per cluster. Hover any cluster to see representative papers.

Who develops edge LMs?

We classified the affiliations of authors across the 58 deployment papers into four sectors: Academia (universities and affiliated research institutions), Industry (startups to enterprise), Research collective (non-profit research organizations), and Government (state-affiliated institutes, public sector). Authors with multiple affiliations are counted in each. Cross-sector collaborations are measured by how often each pair of sectors co-occurs within the same paper.

Recommendations

  1. For NLP researchers and model developers: Evaluate edge models beyond memory (e.g., compute and energy), and explore underrepresented methods since current deployments cluster around a relatively narrow toolkit.
  2. For deployment practitioners and communities at the edge: Build cross-sector collaborations (academia, industry, research collectives, government), and involve local communities as active collaborators in development and deployment.
  3. For policymakers and funders: Invest not only in model development but also in infrastructure and devices that make deployment feasible in linguistically diverse, lower-resource settings; increase public-sector participation in edge LM efforts.

Methodology

To understand the landscape of multilingual and edge LMs, we collect research from January 2021 to March 2026 on multilinguality, edge and efficient NLP, or both. In total, we obtain 306 papers.

Identification of papers

Keyword search. We gather studies from *CL (e.g., ACL, EMNLP, TACL), machine learning venues (e.g., NeurIPS, ICLR, TMLR), related workshops, and preprints using the Semantic Scholar API. Our search query includes keywords pertaining to multilingual NLP, efficiency, and LM deployment. This step resulted in 2,473 papers.

Targeted search. To cover work outside mainstream *CL and ML venues, we perform a targeted search across three groups of venues: regional venues such as AfricaNLP and PACLIC, efficiency-focused workshops such as SustaiNLP and ENLSP, and human-computer interaction and education venues where deployments are commonly reported, such as CHI and AIED. We also include grey literature such as technical reports and model cards that describe deployed systems. Within these sources, we include papers that study a non-English language, a hardware constraint, or a real-world deployment. This step resulted in 55 papers.

Screening and annotation of papers

Citation filter. We screen the keyword search results by citation count in a staggered manner, with thresholds by publication year. We apply this filter to keep annotation tractable, as the keyword search returns thousands of papers. In addition, the targeted search results are exempted from this filter so as to accommodate relevant works that may be disadvantaged by this criterion. Combined, this step led to an initial set of 1,069 papers for annotation.

LM-assisted annotation. In order to characterize each work, we annotate each paper across several dimensions. We bootstrapped all annotations using GPT-4.1 Mini, prompting the model with each paper's title and abstract.

  • Pipeline stage (multi-label): which stage(s) of the LM pipeline the work contributes to: Data Collection, Pretraining, Post-training, Inference, Evaluation, or Full-Stack.
  • Focus (single-label): whether the work specifically targets multilinguality or edge efficiency, or both.
  • Deployment device and mode: the target hardware or device for deployment, if specified, and whether a work was deployed on-device or as a client.
  • Deployment (yes/no): whether an actual deployment has taken place, i.e., any mention of usage or community-based evaluation or feedback.
  • Artifacts: for papers that release a model or dataset, its name, parameter count (if model), and whether it was used for training or evaluation (if dataset).

Post-correction. We conduct a round of post-correction to verify the LM-bootstrapped annotations. We check whether the labels are correct given the title and abstract; if they appear incorrect or ambiguous, then we consult the full paper and make a correction. During this step, we also exclude papers that (1) address neither multilinguality nor efficiency, (2) do not involve language as a modality (e.g., vision-only work), or (3) have no technical contribution such as surveys and position papers. This reduces the set to 306 papers.

Included papers in the survey

These 306 works range from peer-reviewed publications to workshops, regional venues, and grey literature. Out of the 306 papers, 6.9% are model releases, 74.2% describe methodologies for developing these edge LMs, while the rest (58 papers, 19.0%) are real-world system deployments.
